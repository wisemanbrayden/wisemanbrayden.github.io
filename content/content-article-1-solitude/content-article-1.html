CREATIVE NONFICTION

The Roads Not Taken

Self-loathing, sticky fingers, nostalgia and bad habits. We make sacrifices to keep ourselves safe — is it worth it?

Photo by Cara Denison on Pexels

by Brayden Wiseman

Published Nov. 17, 2026

I thought I was an extrovert growing up.

I envied my friends who seemed comfortable outside of routine. For years, I tried to emulate the ease with which they lived within the moment, and not the future or the past.

There came a time though, in 2016, when the guise split apart at the seams. I figured I could fake it 'til I made it, and I did for nearly a decade.

Back then, my friends and I liked to get together most weekends. We'd rotate between a few suburban basements in a hillside neighborhood off Redwood Road, about 25 minutes south of Salt Lake City.

There were eight of us, sometimes seven or six or four. We'd play Ticket to Ride or Spoons, fueled by Solo cup Mountain Dew and stale Great Value donuts.

God, I needed it.

Amidst an otherwise crusty-eyed high school tenure, I remember the nights when my laughter pitched higher until my eyes welled and stung, until my obliques began to ache. It helped me through an early-life nadir.

But as they often do, things turned sour with my high school sweetheart, and those friendships withered in turn. I could have salvaged some with a little effort. It hurts that they fell to such pathetic disrepair.

Why were simple things hard? And why did the messy and caked, lingering glaze of cheap pastries stick to my fingers like tar?

One night, we were all hanging out in a too-small living room connected to a cramped kitchen and an even smaller entryway.

A few hours in, I feigned a phone call and excused myself, lying "hello" to nobody as I pulled the front door shut behind me. I pocketed my phone after rounding the street corner.

It was December, I think, and the evening sun was long retired behind the Wasatch Front. The cold wasn't comfortable. The suburban gutters were lined with asphalt-tinged sludge and slurry. The Utah Valley's winter air was turbid and subtly toxic, but I never carried my inhaler back then.

I returned about 20 minutes later — recharged, red-nosed and wheezing. Steeling myself on the porch.

The shitty trudge through the snow was unremarkable, but I recall the melancholic, upsetting revelation that struck me when I re-entered the home and sat to remove my wet Nikes.

The contrast was travesty. Shivering through the silent neighborhood streets, I'd felt the beat of my heart in my ears, each exhale only quieter than the dry crunch of unhurried steps on packed snow.

Once inside, my pale skin flushed with red in the heat of the entryway. My shaking fingers and toes grew swollen and useless. Jackets and bags piled near the coat closet, and cacophonous voices echoed down the hallway, accompanied by Mario Party sound effects and somebody's splintering tenor rendition of The Chainsmokers' Closer.

I'd never know what I missed in those 20 minutes. I liked my friends very much, and that made me sad.

I struggle to reconcile that, still. I crave human connection on unfair terms. I want more, but not all at once; I need more, but I need my space.

Again, again, again and again, I eschew easy pathways, shake my head at the simple solutions. To my dismay, I take the well-trodden road — the sludge-guttered street that I walk just to breathe.
